## [OpenPrinter: Open-Source Printer Concept Sparks Skepticism on HN](https://www.opentools.studio/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

OpenPrinter is a proposed open-source consumer printer project currently at the pre-crowdfunding landing page stage, promising freedom from proprietary ink cartridges and DRM subscriptions. The project has attracted significant Hacker News attention (746 points, 180 comments) but has not yet demonstrated working hardware or actual printing functionality. The project taps into widespread consumer frustration with proprietary printers that lock users into expensive ink subscriptions and use DRM to block third-party cartridges. If successful, it could become a landmark right-to-repair hardware product, though skeptics question whether a small team can realistically overcome the enormous engineering and materials science challenges that have kept the inkjet market dominated by a few large incumbents. The project is licensed under Creative Commons BY-NC-SA 4.0, whose non-commercial clause arguably makes it not truly open-source by conventional definitions. Key technical questions raised by commenters include the complexity of print head engineering, ink chemistry, and paper handling mechanics — the last being surprisingly difficult even before print quality is considered.

hackernews · bouh · Jul 5, 21:03 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48797916)

**Background**: Inkjet printers have been consumer products for over 40 years, but the market is dominated by a handful of manufacturers (HP, Canon, Epson, Brother) that rely heavily on proprietary ink cartridges and increasingly on subscription services like HP Instant Ink. Modern color printers also include hidden yellow dot tracking patterns (Machine Identification Code) that governments use to trace printed documents. Open-source hardware efforts in complex consumer categories like this have historically struggled due to the deep expertise, capital, and supply chain relationships required for competitive manufacturing.

**Discussion**: Community sentiment is mixed: supporters argue the team is assembling existing modules rather than inventing inkjet technology from scratch, and see a real market for repairable, subscription-free printers. Skeptics counter that inkjet engineering demands orders of magnitude more materials science and industry expertise than enthusiasts realize, and point out concrete concerns like paper handling, absence of a printing demo, the restrictive non-commercial license, and even the legal problem of the government-mandated yellow tracking dots.

## [The Real Gaming Issue: Ownership Rights, Not Physical vs. Digital](https://popcar.bearblog.dev/its-about-ownership/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A widely-discussed blog post argues that the gaming community's debate over physical versus digital media misses the real point: what matters is whether players actually own what they buy, with the author noting that Steam still allows relatively unrestricted ownership while many other platforms strip away basic consumer rights. As digital storefronts, subscription services, and always-online DRM become the industry norm, consumers increasingly find that purchases can be revoked, delisted, or rendered unplayable, raising fundamental questions about property rights in the digital age. The debate has policy implications extending beyond gaming to all digital media including music, films, and books. The author emphasizes that Steam does not enforce hard DRM and games can typically run offline without the launcher, while contrasting this with Ubisoft Connect and other platforms that tie games to persistent online services. Physical media is also increasingly compromised, as many modern console games ship as mere installation stubs requiring large downloads and account authentication.

hackernews · popcar2 · Jul 5, 14:56 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48794750)

**Background**: DRM (Digital Rights Management) is technology used by publishers to control how software is used, often requiring online authentication or launchers like Ubisoft Connect, EA App, or Denuvo. Under most modern End User License Agreements, buyers receive only a revocable license rather than actual ownership, which contrasts with the first-sale doctrine that traditionally protects physical purchases. Recent industry shifts—including Sony's plans to phase out physical discs by 2028 and Ubisoft's controversial removal of purchased games like The Crew—have intensified concerns about consumers' inability to preserve or transfer their digital libraries.
